\begin{abstract}
Accurate segmentation of plant structures is essential for image-based
phenotyping, but many current workflows need command-line skills, powerful
computing, or manually annotated training data, limiting use in small labs,
early-stage studies, and non-model crops. We present \textit{LeafSegmenter}, an
open-source, cross-platform desktop tool for interactive plant image
segmentation and phenotyping with the Segment Anything Model 2 (SAM~2). The
application combines foundation-model segmentation with plant-specific image
enhancement, colour-guided mask filtering, interactive mask editing, geometric
and learned leaf completion, folded-leaf unfolding, phenotype measurement, and
lightweight custom-model training. LeafSegmenter runs on CPUs and can use GPU
acceleration via CUDA and Apple MPS, enabling segmentation on standard laptops
without high-performance computing. Curated masks can be exported as binary
images, transparent crops, JSON/CSV manifests, and phenotype tables with
morphometric, colour, and vegetation-index traits. Curated SAM~2 masks can also
train lightweight U-Net models for faster, task-specific inference on new
images. Qualitative demonstrations show that LeafSegmenter supports diverse
phenotyping workflows, including image enhancement, SAM~2 parameter tuning,
manual boundary correction, partial leaf completion, and stress-specific tip
segmentation. By combining foundation-model segmentation with practical
human-in-the-loop correction and phenotype export, LeafSegmenter lowers the
barrier to scalable plant image analysis and offers a flexible platform for
generating quantitative traits for genetic, physiological, and breeding studies.

\textbf{Keywords:} plant phenotyping; image segmentation; Segment Anything Model;
SAM~2; leaf morphology; image-based phenotyping; QTL mapping; genome-wide
association studies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Plant phenotyping is the quantitative measurement of plant morphology, architecture, color, and growth-related traits. It is central to modern crop improvement, stress physiology, and functional plant biology \citep{walter2015plant,furbank2011phenomics}. As genotyping has become faster and cheaper, precise, high-throughput trait measurement has become the main bottleneck in linking genotype, environment, and phenotype
\citep{pound2017deep,singh2016machine}. Digital imaging partly addresses this by enabling non-destructive measurements in controlled environments, greenhouses, field plots, and UAV platforms, but the bottleneck has shifted from image acquisition to image interpretation. Accurate segmentation of plant organs from backgrounds, soil, trays, shadows, neighboring plants, and overlapping tissues remains essential for reliable trait extraction.

A segmentation model can be difficult to train because plants vary widely across species, genotypes, developmental stages, imaging platforms, and environments. Leaves can overlap, fold, curl, or occlude each other; field images often have uneven illumination and complex backgrounds; and color differences between target tissues and other objects, such as grass and weeds, can be grass and subtle. Classical methods based on color thresholding, vegetation indices, and morphological operations can work under controlled conditions but often need extensive parameter tuning and fail when lighting, background, or morphology changes \citep{woebbecke1995color,gitelson2002novel}. Deep learning methods, such as U-Net-style semantic segmentation and Mask R-CNN-style instance segmentation, have greatly improved plant segmentation \citep{ronneberger2015unet,he2017mask,pound2017deep}, but supervised models still require large annotated datasets, task-specific training, and
computational expertise, limiting their use in small laboratories, early-stage projects, and scenarios with scarce annotated data.

Visual foundation models create new opportunities for plant phenotyping. Trained on large, diverse datasets, they can often be adapted to new tasks with little or no task-specific training \citep{bommasani2021opportunities}. The Segment Anything Model (SAM) introduced promptable segmentation, where users guide the model with points, boxes, or masks to obtain object masks in a zero-shot setting \citep{kirillov2023segment}. SAM~2 extends this to images and videos using a promptable segmentation architecture, hierarchical image encoder, and streaming memory, enabling improved interactive segmentation and refinement \citep{ravi2025sam2}. These models are useful for plant phenotyping because many studies need accurate masks but lack resources to annotate and train a new model for each species, organ, or imaging condition.

Applications of SAM to plant phenotyping has shown both promise and limitations. \citet{zhang2024adapting} combined SAM with image–text prompting for zero-shot plant recognition and phenotypic measurement without manually labeled training data. \citet{williams2024leafonlysam} built a zero-shot pipeline for potato leaf segmentation using SAM plus plant-specific post-processing. Their results show that SAM-based methods are useful when annotated data are scarce, but automatic outputs often include background objects, duplicate masks, whole-plant masks, or non-leaf structures that must be filtered before measurements are reliable. Foundation models thus reduce the annotation burden, but practical plant phenotyping still needs human-in-the-loop correction, mask filtering, quality control, and phenotype-specific measurement tools. Transformer-based vision models capture long-range spatial relationships via self-attention and now underpin modern segmentation systems such as SAM and SAM~2 \citep{dosovitskiy2021image,ravi2025sam2}. Yet they are often computationally expensive and rely on large-scale pretraining or carefully tuned training, whereas convolutional models can remain competitive when data or compute are limited \citep{chen2021visformer}. For routine phenotyping, this creates demand for workflows that pair the accuracy and flexibility of foundation models with lightweight models that can be trained from curated masks and quickly deployed on new images. Knowledge distillation and teacher–student training address this need: high-quality masks generated or corrected by a large model can train a smaller model for faster, task-specific inference \citep{hinton2015distilling,zhang2023mobilesam}.

Despite these advances, accessible software for plant-focused, interactive, foundation-model segmentation remains scarce. General annotation platforms such as LabelMe offer manual labelling but lack plant-specific enhancement, mask filtering, phenotypic measurement, and model training workflows \citep{russell2008labelme}. PlantCV is a powerful, widely used image-analysis library for plant phenotyping, but is mainly script-based and thus requires programming expertise for many tasks \citep{gehan2017plantcv}. Meanwhile, many SAM-based plant studies appear as pipelines or experimental frameworks rather than integrated desktop applications that let non-expert users load images, segment plants, edit masks, export traits, and train a lightweight model within a single environment.

We present \textbf{LeafSegmenter}, an interactive, Python-based desktop application for plant image segmentation and phenotyping with SAM~2 and lightweight companion models. LeafSegmenter is designed to make plant image analysis accessible in everyday biology workflows, especially for users with limited programming experience, few annotations, or no access to high-performance computing. The software combines SAM~2-based segmentation with plant-specific image enhancement, folder-level batch processing, colour-guided mask filtering, interactive mask editing, geometric and learned leaf completion, folded-leaf reconstruction, phenotype export, and integrated training workflows for lightweight custom models. Segmentation outputs can be saved as reusable mask bundles and reloaded later for further curation or measurement. The software is available at \url{https://github.com/nirwan1265/Image-Segmentation}.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Application Architecture}

LeafSegmenter is implemented in Python~3.11 with a \texttt{tkinter}-based graphical interface. The desktop application is organized as a modular package (\texttt{app/}) that separates interface logic from computational routines. The main GUI state and event handling are implemented in \texttt{gui\_app.py}; \texttt{app\_visuals.py} provides theme and reusable interface components; \texttt{image\_processing.py}, \texttt{mask\_utils.py}, and \texttt{phenotyping.py} contain pure image-processing, mask-geometry, and measurement functions; \texttt{sam2\_utils.py} manages SAM~2 loading and Hydra configuration; \texttt{color\_filter.py} implements interactive colour-based filtering; and \texttt{tab\_leaf\_completion.py}, \texttt{tab\_leaf\_unfolding.py}, and \texttt{tab\_train.py} define specialised workflow panels. Lightweight auxiliary training and inference back ends are invoked from the GUI through companion scripts, including \texttt{tip\_segmenter\_model.py}, \texttt{tip\_segmenter\_trainer.py}, and \texttt{mask\_completion.py}.

This modular design separates computationally intensive functions from the GUI, allowing independent testing of image enhancement, mask geometry, and phenotype extraction while keeping the desktop workflow interactive.


\subsection{Image Loading and Preprocessing}

The application accepts TIFF, PNG, JPEG, and BMP images. A built-in TIF converter converts raw field-acquisition formats to standard 8-bit PNG for segmentation. Images are loaded as RGB \texttt{uint8} NumPy arrays via Pillow and OpenCV python packages. A rotation module uses a dial widget with preset angles (45°, 90°, 180°, 270°, $-$45°, $-$90°) and arbitrary rotation to orient images acquired at non-standard angles before segmentation.

In addition to single-image analysis, the application can open whole folders for sequential review and batch segmentation. A preview-coupled crop tool allows users to restrict analysis to a region of interest before segmentation. Previously exported masks can also be restored from JSON or CSV manifests, enabling iterative curation and phenotyping without rerunning model inference.

\subsection{Image Enhancement}

A dedicated enhancement panel offers a configurable, sequential processing pipeline for plant imagery, including these operations:

\subsubsection{Green-Channel CLAHE Enhancement}

The main plant-specific enhancement applies Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalisation (CLAHE) \citep{zuiderveld1994clahe} only to the value channel (V) of pixels classified as green by HSV thresholding. This boosts contrast within leaf tissue while leaving the background unchanged, improving the signal-to-background ratio for SAM~2. A bilateral filter \citep{tomasi1998bilateral} then reduces noise while preserving leaf edges, followed by Sobel edge blending \citep{sobel1968isotropic} to highlight boundaries. An unsharp masking step \citep{dahl2008contrast} concludes the standard plant pipeline.

\subsubsection{Vegetation Indices}

The application computes five common vegetation indices from the RGB channels, usable as visual overlays or input features for downstream analysis:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ExG} (Excess Green): $\mathrm{ExG} = 2G - R - B$
          \citep{woebbecke1995color}
    \item \textbf{ExGR}: $\mathrm{ExGR} = \mathrm{ExG} - 1.4R - G$
    \item \textbf{GRVI} (Green-Red Vegetation Index):
          $\mathrm{GRVI} = (G - R)/(G + R)$ \citep{tucker1979red}
    \item \textbf{VARI} (Visible Atmospherically Resistant Index):
          $\mathrm{VARI} = (G - R)/(G + R - B)$ \citep{gitelson2002novel}
    \item \textbf{GLI} (Green Leaf Index):
          $\mathrm{GLI} = (2G - R - B)/(2G + R + B)$ \citep{louhaichi2001spatially}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Advanced Enhancement Options}

Additional enhancement methods are available for challenging imaging
conditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Retinex} (single- and multi-scale) \citep{jobson1997multiscale}
          for illumination normalisation under variable field lighting.
    \item \textbf{Morphological top-hat transform} \citep{serra1983image} for
          uneven background illumination correction.
    \item \textbf{Non-local means denoising} \citep{buades2005non} for
          high-noise images.
    \item \textbf{Guided filtering} \citep{he2013guided} for edge-preserving
          smoothing using the green channel as guide.
    \item \textbf{LAB colour-space enhancement}, \textbf{grey-world and
          max-white white balance}, \textbf{Difference of Gaussians},
          \textbf{local contrast normalisation}, \textbf{adaptive gamma
          correction}, and \textbf{shadow/highlight recovery}.
    \item \textbf{Background whitening}: HSV-based detection of
          bright, desaturated background pixels (e.g.\ white imaging trays)
          with morphological cleanup, replacing them with white to simplify
          foreground segmentation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{SAM2-Based Segmentation}

PlantSegmenter integrates SAM~2 \citep{ravi2024sam2} via the official Python API. The user selects a SAM~2 checkpoint (e.g., Hiera-Small, Hiera-Large, or a bundled \texttt{.pt} file) and the compute device (CPU, CUDA, or Apple MPS). After image enhancement, segmentation runs on the current image using \texttt{SAM2AutomaticMaskGenerator}, with all hyperparameters configurable in the GUI:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{points\_per\_side}: grid density of prompt points.
    \item \texttt{pred\_iou\_thresh}: predicted IoU quality threshold.
    \item \texttt{stability\_score\_thresh}: mask stability rejection threshold.
    \item \texttt{crop\_n\_layers}: number of multi-scale crop passes.
    \item \texttt{crop\_overlap\_ratio}: overlap between adjacent crops.
    \item \texttt{box\_nms\_thresh}: duplicate suppression threshold.
    \item \texttt{min\_mask\_region\_area}: minimum mask area in pixels.
    \item \texttt{use\_m2m}: mask-to-mask refinement flag.
\end{itemize}

Hover tooltips in the GUI also explain each parameter. After generation, masks are deduplicated using Intersection-over-Union (IoU $> 0.80$) and split into single-connected-component instances. Batch segmentation processes an entire folder, caching results in memory for later review and export. LeafSegmenter accepts either conventional SAM~2 checkpoint--configuration pairs or bundled model files that embed configuration metadata. For folder-level workflows, the application caches per-image segmentation results in memory so users can review images one by one and later export all masks in a single batch-saving step.

\subsection{Mask Editing Tools}

A dedicated Masks panel provides interactive editing operations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Delete}: remove selected masks from the set.
    \item \textbf{Combine}: union of two or more selected masks into one.
    \item \textbf{Refine}: re-run SAM~2 within the bounding box of a selected
          mask using denser prompting for improved boundary accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Edit boundary}: an interactive contour editor allowing
          point-by-point drag adjustment of mask boundaries, with add-point
          (Ctrl+click) and delete-point (Shift+click) operations and optional
          Gaussian smoothing.
    \item \textbf{Split}: decompose multi-blob masks into individual
          connected-component masks.
\end{itemize}

An undo history system (up to 50 states) captures the full mask list before
each destructive operation, enabling recovery via Ctrl+Z (or Cmd+Z on macOS).

\subsection{Color-Based Mask Filtering}

A color filter panel enables interactive mask selection from pixel color statistics. A 2D Hue × Saturation picker renders the HSV space as a gradient, where users drag rectangles to define color ranges combined with OR logic. An eyedropper samples pixels from the preview canvas, places a crosshair on the picker, and auto-creates a selection rectangle with suitable tolerance. A global brightness (Value) range control applies to all selections. The filter uses the mean HSV color of each mask’s pixels, allowing fast removal of soil, background, or specular highlight masks without manual selection.

\subsection{Leaf Shape Completion}
\label{sec:completion}

Many plant images contain partially occluded or truncated leaves. To enable complete leaf reconstruction for morphometric analysis, PlantSegmenter includes a leaf completion module with two complementary shape galleries:

\subsubsection{Geometric Shape Gallery}

Eighteen botanical leaf forms—Orbicular, Elliptical, Ovate, Obovate, Cordate, Reniform, Lanceolate, Oblanceolate, Oblong, Linear, Ensiform, Cuneate, Spathulate, Deltoid, Rhomboid, Sagittate, Hastate, and a general Ellipse—are provided as silhouette thumbnails defined by parametric polygons.

\subsubsection{Crop Species Gallery}

Eighteen crop-specific silhouettes are provided, traced from documented leaf morphology: Maize, Wheat, Sorghum, Rice, and Barley (narrow strap-leaf monocots); Arabidopsis (rosette); Tomato and Potato (pinnate compound); Soybean (trifoliate); Sunflower, Tobacco, Cassava (palmate); and Cotton, Grape, Common Bean, Cucumber, Pepper, and Lettuce.

\subsubsection{Alignment Algorithm}

Given a selected mask and shape template, the completion algorithm performs
three sequential alignment steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Centroid alignment}: the shape template is translated so that
          its centre coincides with the pixel centre of mass (centroid) of the
          mask.
    \item \textbf{PCA orientation}: Principal Component Analysis is applied to
          the mask pixel coordinates.  The major eigenvector defines the leaf's
          primary axis; the template is rotated to align its major axis with
          this direction.
    \item \textbf{Scale fitting}: the template axes are scaled to match the
          PCA-derived major and minor axis lengths of the mask.
\end{enumerate}

The aligned template is rasterised at full resolution and merged with the original mask (preserving all pixels). Fine interactive adjustments to scale, position, and rotation are available before committing the final mask.


\subsubsection{Learned Mask Completion}

In addition to template-based completion, LeafSegmenter supports a learned mask-to-mask completion model. This model takes a cropped partial binary mask as input and predicts a completed binary mask without using RGB information. Training uses complete leaf masks as ground truth and synthesizes partial masks on the fly using three occlusion strategies: edge truncation, random geometric occluders, and leaf-on-leaf overlays sampled from other masks in the training set. Random flips, rotations, aspect-ratio perturbations, and scale changes are used as augmentation. The completion network is a compact one-channel U-Net trained with a combined binary cross-entropy plus Dice loss, AdamW optimisation, gradient clipping, and cosine-annealed learning rate. During inference, the selected mask is cropped to its bounding box with padding, completed in crop coordinates, mapped back to full-image coordinates, and restricted to connected components that touch the original mask.


\subsection{Leaf Unfolding Reconstruction}
\label{sec:unfolding}

Folded leaves are common in greenhouse and field imagery, especially for maize, sorghum, and wheat. A Leaf Unfolding module reconstructs the full leaf by mirroring the folded part across an automatically detected fold line.

The fold line is found as the boundary between two selected masks representing the folded halves. Both masks are morphologically dilated, and their intersection defines a junction zone. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) on the junction pixel coordinates yields the fold line direction as the first right singular vector. Each pixel $\mathbf{p}$ of the folded mask is then reflected as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{p}' = \mathbf{p} - 2\left[(\mathbf{p} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \cdot
    \hat{\mathbf{n}}\right]\hat{\mathbf{n}}
    \label{eq:reflection}
\end{equation}

where $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is a point on the fold line and $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ is its unit normal. The reflected mask is morphologically closed to remove rounding artefacts, and the corresponding image pixels are mapped to their reflected locations. Before applying, the user can interactively adjust the fold line angle (in $\pm$5° steps) and shift the mirrored piece.

\subsection{Phenotype Measurement and Export}

For each mask (or set of selected masks), the following measurements are
computed and exported as CSV files:

\subsubsection{Morphometric Descriptors}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Area} (px$^2$)
    \item \textbf{Length and width}: PCA major/minor axis lengths; deskewed
          row-span length and 95th-percentile row width.
    \item \textbf{Perimeter}, \textbf{convex hull area and perimeter}
    \item \textbf{Solidity}: mask area / convex hull area
    \item \textbf{Circularity}: $4\pi A / P^2$
    \item \textbf{Extent}: mask area / bounding box area
    \item \textbf{Equivalent diameter}: diameter of a circle of equal area
    \item \textbf{Connected components}: number of distinct blobs
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Color Descriptors}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Per-channel (R, G, B, H, S, V) mean, median, sum, and standard
          deviation within the mask
    \item HSV channel variances
    \item Vegetation index means: ExG, ExR, ExGR, GLI, green pixel fraction
\end{itemize}

Both \textit{individual} (per-segment) and \textit{joint} (aggregated) CSV exports are supported. Masks can be saved as full-size binary PNGs, bounding-box-cropped binary masks, or RGBA transparent-background crops. A combined export also writes JSON or CSV manifests linking masks to the source image and segmentation preview, enabling later reloading for editing or measurement. For geometrically extended masks, geometric descriptors use the completed mask, while colour-based statistics use only observed pixels, excluding synthetic extensions when possible.

\subsection{Custom Model Training}
\label{sec:training}

To enable fast class-specific segmentation without SAM~2 at inference, LeafSegmenter includes a workflow for training lightweight binary segmentation models from curated masks.

\subsubsection{Data Collection}

Users collect training examples during routine curation. Selected masks are exported to a structured dataset with images in \texttt{images/} and binary instance masks in \texttt{masks/}. Multiple instance masks for the same image are merged during training into a single target mask. Images without the target class can be marked with \texttt{\_nomask.txt} and used as negative examples.

\subsubsection{Model Architecture}

The GUI exposes two binary segmentation architectures: a compact custom U-Net (\texttt{unet\_small}) and a U-Net-style decoder with a ResNet-18 encoder (\texttt{unet\_resnet18}), optionally pretrained on ImageNet. Both output a single-channel probability map, which is thresholded to produce a binary mask.

\subsubsection{Training Procedure}

Training images and masks are letterboxed to a fixed square size with preserved aspect ratio. The pipeline applies lightweight augmentation (horizontal/vertical flips and mild brightness–contrast jitter). Foreground imbalance is addressed via a positive-class weight estimated from the curated dataset and a loss combining weighted binary cross-entropy with Dice. Optimisation uses AdamW with configurable learning rate, batch size, device, input size, and training steps, with optional checkpoint resume. Training progress is streamed to the GUI log in real time.

\subsubsection{Inference}

At inference, the trained model processes arbitrary-resolution RGB images using the same letterbox/unletterbox strategy. The probability map is thresholded and post-processed by connected-component analysis, minimum-area filtering, and optional retention of only the largest component. For large images, a sliding-window mode runs the model on overlapping tiles and merges the outputs. Optional HSV-guided tile screening can skip clearly irrelevant regions, and additional post-filters can suppress white background pixels or remove green tissue, depending on the target class.

\section*{Results}

We present qualitative results illustrating the main LeafSegmenter workflows as follows: SAM~2-based segmentation, image enhancement, interactive mask editing, leaf completion, and lightweight custom-model training.


\subsection*{Image Enhancement Improves Leaf Contrast and Segmentation Readiness}

Image enhancement altered contrast, colour balance, and edge visibility in ways that can simplify segmentation, depending on the input image (Fig.~\ref{fig:image_enhancement}). The original image is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:image_enhancement}A, with representative outputs including green-aware enhancement (B), edge darkening (C), Retinex-based illumination correction (D), ExG vegetation-index enhancement (E), and LAB-based enhancement (F). Each mode emphasizes different features: some highlight leaf boundaries, others increase contrast between green tissue and background, and others correct uneven lighting or colour imbalance. Instead of a single fixed preprocessing method, LeafSegmenter lets users choose the enhancement strategy that best improves plant visibility for each scene, enabling more effective segmentation.

\subsection*{SAM~2 Parameters Define Mask Density and Boundary Delineation}

Segmentation output depended strongly on SAM~2 mask-generation settings, particularly \texttt{points\_per\_side} and \texttt{pred\_iou\_thresh} (Fig.~\ref{fig:sam2_params}). With \texttt{pred\_iou\_thresh} fixed at 0.8, increasing \texttt{points\_per\_side} from 8 to 32 yielded more detected masks and finer subdivision of plant and background regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:sam2_params}A,B). With \texttt{points\_per\_side} fixed at 32, reducing \texttt{pred\_iou\_thresh} from 0.8 to 0.2 retained additional masks, including smaller and lower-confidence regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:sam2_params}B,C). Thus, lower sampling density and stricter IoU filtering produced fewer, more conservative masks, whereas denser sampling and lower IoU thresholds yielded more exhaustive but noisier segments. LeafSegmenter can therefore be tuned for either cleaner mask selection or maximal segment recovery, depending on the image and downstream objective.

\subsection*{Interactive Boundary Editing Improves Local Mask Accuracy}

Automatic segmentation can be refined using LeafSegmenter’s interactive mask-editing tools (Fig.~\ref{fig:mask_editing}). In the example, the auricle region of a maize leaf was identified and then corrected by directly editing the mask boundary: control points were dragged to better follow tissue margins and extend the segment toward the stem, yielding a more complete, biologically accurate mask (Fig.~\ref{fig:mask_editing}A--C). This user-guided boundary adjustment corrects local under-segmentation or shape mismatch without rerunning the full workflow and is especially useful when automatic masks only partially capture the target structure or miss fine morphological boundaries.

\subsection*{Leaf Completion Restores Plausible Leaf Shape for Area Estimation}

Leaf completion reconstructs a more complete leaf outline when a target leaf is occluded or only partly segmented (Fig.~\ref{fig:leaf_completion}). In the Arabidopsis example, default segmentation detected a partially visible rosette leaf, which was then selected by its bounding box for completion (Fig.~\ref{fig:leaf_completion}A). A geometric template from the built-in leaf-shape gallery was aligned to the visible mask and used to generate a completed outline (Fig.~\ref{fig:leaf_completion}B,C). When mapped back to the full segmented image, the completed mask yielded a fuller, more biologically plausible leaf shape (Fig.~\ref{fig:leaf_completion}D). This workflow mitigates underestimation of leaf area due to overlap or partial visibility, enabling morphometric measurements that better approximate true leaf dimensions.

\subsection*{Custom U-Net Training Enables Stress-Specific Tip Segmentation}

LeafSegmenter enabled training of a lightweight custom segmentation model for a specific stress-phenotyping task in young maize leaves (Fig.~\ref{fig:custom_model}). Cold-stressed leaves with white to yellowish tip tissue were first identified and segmented using the interactive SAM~2-assisted workflow (Fig.~\ref{fig:custom_model}A). The curated tip masks then served as training targets for a custom U-Net model with encoder-based feature extraction (Fig.~\ref{fig:custom_model}B). After training, the model segmented stress-damaged tip tissue in new images while ignoring healthy leaf blade tissue (Fig.~\ref{fig:custom_model}C), showing how LeafSegmenter-derived masks can be converted into a smaller, task-specific model for rapid, specialised inference.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

LeafSegmenter was developed to bridge the gap between recent visual foundation
models and practical plant phenotyping. SAM and SAM~2 offer powerful promptable
segmentation that reduces reliance on large labelled datasets
\citep{kirillov2023segment,ravi2025sam2}, but using them directly still demands
computational setup, programming skills, weight management, and post-processing
before biological traits can be extracted. This is a key barrier in plant
biology, where users must segment diverse species, organs, developmental stages,
and imaging conditions rather than a single object class. LeafSegmenter
addresses this by combining SAM~2 with an interactive desktop interface,
plant-specific enhancement tools, mask filtering, manual correction, phenotype
export, and lightweight model training in a single workflow.

The qualitative results show that no single segmentation setting or
preprocessing strategy works best for all plant images. Enhancement methods such
as green-aware contrast adjustment, Retinex correction, ExG-based enhancement,
and LAB-space adjustment highlight different image properties and can improve
segmentation under different lighting and background conditions. SAM~2
parameters likewise shape mask density and boundary quality: higher prompt
densities and lower predicted-IoU thresholds yield more candidate masks but more
noise, whereas stricter settings produce fewer, cleaner segments. This
tunability is valuable because phenotyping tasks differ: some require maximal
recovery of all visible tissue, while others need fewer, high-confidence masks
that require minimal correction.

LeafSegmenter treats foundation-model outputs as editable phenotyping objects rather than final measurements. Prior SAM-based plant studies show that zero-shot segmentation is useful when annotations are limited, but automatic masks can include background, duplicates, whole-plant masks, or non-target structures \citep{zhang2024adapting,williams2024leafonlysam}. LeafSegmenter therefore provides colour-based filtering, mask deletion and combination, boundary editing, mask splitting, refinement, and undo history for human-in-the-loop quality control. This is crucial for biological measurements where small mask errors affect area, shape, colour, or stress estimates. The boundary-editing workflow lets users fix local under-segmentation without rerunning the model, balancing automation and expert curation.

The leaf-completion and unfolding modules address common phenotyping biases beyond standard segmentation. In field and greenhouse images, leaves are often occluded, truncated, folded, curled, or overlapping, so using only the visible mask underestimates area and distorts shape descriptors. Geometric template-based and learned mask-completion workflows estimate plausible full-leaf shape from partial masks, while the unfolding module uses mirror reflection across a detected fold line to reconstruct folded leaves. These tools do not replace ground-truth measurements; completed regions are model-assisted estimates, not observed tissue. Downstream analyses should therefore retain metadata indicating whether each mask was directly observed, manually edited, geometrically completed, learned completed, or unfolded. These modules nonetheless help reduce systematic underestimation of leaf traits when perfect visibility is impossible.

LeafSegmenter supports transitioning from interactive foundation-model segmentation to faster task-specific inference. Curated SAM~2 masks can serve as training targets for lightweight U-Net models, enabling users to turn a small set of corrected examples into a deployable model for repeated analysis of similar images. This teacher–student workflow distills high-quality outputs from a large model into a smaller one for efficient inference \citep{hinton2015distilling,zhang2023mobilesam}. In the stress-tip example, curated masks from cold-stressed maize leaves trained a custom model that segmented damaged tip tissue while ignoring healthy leaf blade tissue. This illustrates how LeafSegmenter can support specialized phenotyping tasks that generic plant masks handle poorly, including stress injury, senescence, disease lesions, nutrient-deficiency symptoms, and organ-specific morphology.

A current limitation is that the results are primarily qualitative. The examples show that the software supports segmentation, enhancement, editing, completion, and custom-model training, but they do not yet establish quantitative accuracy across species, imaging platforms, or phenotyping tasks. Future benchmarking should compare LeafSegmenter-derived masks and traits with expert annotations and manual measurements across diverse crops, including monocots with narrow leaves, dicots with broad or compound leaves, rosette plants, and field-grown plants with complex backgrounds. Key evaluation metrics include mask-level precision, recall, Dice coefficient, intersection-over-union, trait-level correlation with manual measurements, processing time, and user-effort reduction. Such validation will clarify when SAM~2-assisted segmentation is sufficient, when manual correction is needed, and when a trained lightweight model is preferable for high-throughput deployment.


\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

LeafSegmenter is an accessible desktop tool for plant image segmentation and phenotyping that integrates SAM~2, image enhancement, interactive mask curation, leaf completion, folded-leaf reconstruction, phenotype export, and lightweight model training. It is designed for plant biologists without extensive programming skills or high-performance computing access. By enabling users to generate, correct, save, reload, measure, and reuse segmentation masks, LeafSegmenter links foundation-model segmentation with routine phenotyping.

A key future direction is using LeafSegmenter for genetic mapping and population-scale phenotyping. It already exports structured morphometric, colour, and vegetation-index traits (area, length, width, perimeter, solidity, circularity, extent, RGB/HSV statistics, ExG, ExGR, GLI, green-pixel fraction), which can serve as quantitative phenotypes for QTL mapping and GWAS. For instance, LeafSegmenter can phenotype segregating populations or diversity panels for leaf area and shape, auricle size, lesion area, chlorosis, senescence, cold-stress tip damage, and growth-stage-specific organ morphology. Combined with genotype data, these traits could reveal genomic regions controlling plant architecture, stress response, disease resistance, and developmental variation.

Future versions of LeafSegmenter should emphasize reproducible
population-level workflows. Key additions include project-level metadata
management, genotype and plot-ID integration, batch phenotype summaries,
quality-control flags, calibration to physical units, and direct export formats
for QTL and GWAS pipelines. For genetic studies, it will also be important to
preserve mask provenance—whether each phenotype comes from an automatic SAM~2
mask, a manually edited mask, a completed mask, an unfolded mask, or a
custom-model prediction. This will let researchers filter or model phenotype
uncertainty before association mapping. With these extensions, LeafSegmenter
could function not only as a segmentation tool, but also as a bridge between
raw plant images and genetic discovery.
